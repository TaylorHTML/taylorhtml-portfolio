CASE STUDY / 03MOBILE COMMERCE

OKAYVELO

Designing confidence into a high-consideration purchase.

A mobile bike-shopping experience that makes comparison easier, keeps checkout moving, and places reassurance where commitment feels highest.

01Challenge

A bike is not an impulse buy. The interface had to respect the weight of the decision.

High-ticket e-commerce creates two connected problems: shoppers need enough information to choose well, then enough confidence to complete the transaction. Solving only the checkout would leave the harder decision untouched.

THE USER PROBLEM

“Which bike is actually right for me?”

Specifications lived on separate pages, important differences were hard to retain, and too much detail made narrowing a choice harder.

THE BUSINESS RISK

Uncertainty compounds near payment.

Account gates, weak delivery context, and invisible reassurance gave shoppers more reasons to leave at the most valuable point in the flow.

HOW MIGHT WEHelp riders move from “I’m still comparing” to “I’m ready to buy” without rushing either decision?
SCOPE

I focused on the critical path from browsing through confirmation. The concept was evaluated as a prototype; the goal was to validate comprehension and confidence, not claim production conversion impact.

02Research

I studied the moments where familiar commerce patterns either reduce effort—or add doubt.

I combined secondary research with walkthroughs of Trek, Amazon, and Target. The audit compared product discovery, comparison support, account requirements, checkout progression, and trust signals.

03Synthesis

Research only matters when it changes the interface.

Three patterns became design decisions. Each one connects an observed friction point to a specific product response.

01

Comparison was improvised

SIGNAL

Shoppers bounced between product pages and tried to remember meaningful differences.

DESIGN RESPONSE

Create one side-by-side view and cap it at two bikes so the decision stays readable on mobile.

02

Commitment arrived too early

SIGNAL

Mandatory account creation added a new decision at the exact moment shoppers were ready to pay.

DESIGN RESPONSE

Lead with guest checkout and make account creation an optional benefit—not a gate.

03

Trust cues were easy to miss

SIGNAL

Security, warranty, and delivery information only helped when it appeared near the decision it supported.

DESIGN RESPONSE

Place reassurance inside comparison, cart, payment, and confirmation—not in a distant policy page.

04Exploration

The first job was proving the journey—not polishing the screens.

Low-fidelity wireframes mapped one continuous path from discovery to purchase. This made it possible to challenge the information order, comparison model, and checkout sequence before visual design raised the cost of change.

DISCOVER→NARROW→COMPARE→CHECK OUT→CONFIRM
05Visual system

Technical enough for the product. Simple enough for the purchase.

Space Grotesk, a focused blue spectrum, direct labeling, and consistent controls give Okay Velo a precise identity while preserving the predictability people rely on in commerce.

06Testing

The first round exposed where the design looked clear—but did not yet behave clearly.

I ran two rounds of prototype testing. Round one identified breakdowns in comparison state, specification density, and the visibility of reassurance. I revised those moments, then used a five-participant second round to check whether the changes held.

01
WHAT I SAW

People expected selected bikes to appear immediately.

WHAT I CHANGED

Made compare status visible and turned selection into a clear, persistent system state.

02
WHAT I SAW

The first specification table felt dense and encouraged skimming.

WHAT I CHANGED

Reduced simultaneous detail, strengthened category hierarchy, and kept the comparison to two bikes.

03
WHAT I SAW

Existing trust cues were overlooked during the first checkout test.

WHAT I CHANGED

Moved security and delivery reassurance closer to payment and made the guest path unmistakable.

ROUND 02 / VALIDATION5/5

participants completed guest checkout without assistance.

The revised compare flow worked as expected, and trust cues were noticed earlier in checkout.Qualitative prototype finding — not a production conversion metric
07Final experience

One connected journey from curiosity to confirmation.

The final prototype supports the full decision arc: discover an option, narrow the field, compare two bikes, check out as a guest, and leave with a clear confirmation.

01Two-bike comparison

A deliberate limit keeps differences legible on a small screen.

02Guest-first checkout

Purchase intent is not interrupted by an account requirement.

03Contextual trust

Security and delivery details appear beside the decisions they support.

08Reflection

Trust is not a badge. It is the cumulative result of understandable decisions.

Okay Velo taught me that established commerce patterns rarely need reinvention. They need sharper prioritization, better timing, and clearer relationships between information and action.

WHAT I WOULD TEST NEXT

Real inventory, real tradeoffs.

Connect the prototype to representative product data, test specification comprehension across rider experience levels, and validate delivery expectations with realistic edge cases.

WHAT I WOULD MEASURE

Behavior beyond usability.

In production, I would track compare usage, checkout entry, guest completion, field errors, abandonment by step, and post-purchase support questions.

THE LIMIT OF THIS STUDY

Confidence was observed—not monetized.

Prototype testing supports the clarity of the revised flow. It does not establish revenue or conversion lift, which would require live behavioral data.
