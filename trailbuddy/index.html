01The brief

The uncertainty starts before the trail does.

A solo hike is a series of decisions: which trail, what to pack, who to tell, and what to do if the plan changes.

Hikers described stitching that plan together across trail apps, checklists, and messages to friends. I wanted to bring those disconnected moments into a more coherent experience.

The challenge: support a hiker’s independence while making preparation and on-trail support easier to find.

Before

Am I ready?

Route, conditions, gear, and a contact.

During

What if the plan changes?

Navigation, progress, and safety tools.

After

How do I close the loop?

A clear finish and a way to reflect.

Project scope

I owned research, synthesis, flows, wireframes, visual design, prototyping, and usability testing. TrailBuddy is a concept prototype; field performance and emergency connectivity were outside the scope of this study.

Independence. With a little reassurance.

I combined solo-hiker interviews with a review of AllTrails, Gaia GPS, and Garmin inReach. An empathy map and the Solo Trekker Sam persona helped turn those findings into priorities.

04Testing & iteration

The useful findings were the moments of hesitation.

I tested the core tasks of planning, starting, and ending a hike, then refined the prototype across two rounds. Three decisions show how the feedback changed the experience.

What changed / 01

A back arrow is not a finish line.

What I observed
Several participants reached for the back button when they wanted to end their hike. The navigation was competing with the task.
What I changed
I removed the back arrow from the trail menu, made Continue Hike and End Hike the explicit choices, and kept a confirmation before completion.

Finishing an activity should be a deliberate action, with a clear next state.

06Results & reflection

Clarity was the most valuable feature.

What round two told me

A clearer core journey.

Participants found the core hike flow clear and easy to follow. The revised review experience, including stars and photos, received positive feedback.

A small hesitation remained on the hike screen, and some participants expected more trail choices and more interactive reviews.

These are qualitative prototype findings, not measured improvements in real-world hiking safety or live-product performance.

What I took away

A familiar control can still mean the wrong thing.

The back-button behavior was a useful reminder: a screen can look straightforward and still leave the next action unclear. Testing the whole journey exposed what a visual review would have missed.

I would now give completion, cancellation, and recovery states the same attention as the main action.

Where I would take it next

  1. 01

    Test outside.

    Check readability and controls in sunlight, in motion, and with one hand.

  2. 02

    Define the offline behavior.

    Work through map downloads, delayed check-ins, and what SOS can actually do without a connection.

  3. 03

    Revisit the long preparation screen.

    Test whether grouping or progressive disclosure improves readiness without hiding essential details.
